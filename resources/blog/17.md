# Juggling Fractal, Revisited
##### September 28, 2026

<img src="resources/images/blog/17-0.png" style="width: 50%;" />

[Previously on this blog,](blog.html?post=11) I had an idea for a physics-based fractal and did a bad job of rendering it. It goes like this: say you run a simulation of a series of balls endlessly dropping onto a pair of tilted platforms.

<img src="resources/images/blog/17-6.gif" style="width: 50%;" />

The balls knock into each other, causing cascading interference. Eventually, all the balls involved in the pattern lose energy and fall away, leaving room for a ball to travel unimpeded and for the behavior to start again. We can find the period of this periodic motion: in the above example, the pattern repeats every 3 balls.

The way the pattern develops relies on the parameters of the simulation, including the angles of the two platforms. With tiny changes to these angles, we can often observe totally different resulting behavior.

<img src="resources/images/blog/17-7.gif" style="width: 100%;" />
<div class="blogcaption">varying the left angle by one thousandth of a degree to yield period 1, 2, and 3</div>

We learned last time that if you want to run precise simulations, Unity's 2D physics engine (Box2D) isn't the tool for the job. Over the last few years, I've taken a couple swings at a smarter approach, and one of those swings finally connected. I'm going to show off some cool pictures, see how my conjectures from last time fared, then I'll explain my approach, and at the end I will reveal my hubristic and karmically doomed attempt to revolutionize the nonexistent field of symbolic physics.

## Expansion shading

Our renders so far feature solid colors and hard edges between regions: not surprising, since they represent periods, which are integers. No smooth gradients for us... right?

Not so fast! Not only can we track collisions between balls; we can also track *near-collisions* between balls. When one ball just barely misses another, if the resulting collision would have bridged a gap between disparate sets of balls, we know it would also have increased the period of the simulation. In this way, we can classify a period-5 simulation as a period-5.5 simulation, or a period-5.9 simulation, depending on exactly how close this near-miss was.

Now, this is oversimplifying matters. These missed collisions would increase the period, but due to the fractal nature of the simulation, we can't really say by how much. Let's ignore that for now and see what it looks like.

<img src="resources/images/blog/17-3.png" style="width: 80%;"/>

The surest sign we've picked the right criterion for these gradients is that they follow the curves of the unshaded diagram. Not only that, they also expose previously hidden patterns:

<img src="resources/images/blog/17-4.png" style="width: 80%;"/>

These details added by expansion shading don't exactly *explain* what's going on, but at least they give us a clearer picture. They also show the borders within regions where the pattern changes, but the period does not.

Last time, I made three conjectures about this diagram. (Family of diagrams, really.) I don't have the chops to *prove* any of them, nor do I really know what chops would be required. Instead, I've got evidence for each.

Despite my repeatedly referring to it as a fractal, all I can really do is zoom in and see if we ever stop finding detail:

#### Some simulations are aperiodic

Let's check out the large black blob briefly visible in the ball-radius parameter sweep. Black regions in the diagram represent simulations longer than we've budgeted for, and correspondingly long periods. We can dial up the maximum detectable period from the default of 20. (This makes rendering take a lot longer.)

<img src="resources/images/blog/17-3.gif" style="width: 80%;" />
<div class="blogcaption">max detectable period 50&nbsp;&rarr; 100&nbsp;&rarr; 250&nbsp;&rarr; 500&nbsp;&rarr; 10,000</div>

So this region of periods longer than detectable barely shrank as we increased our max period. It sure looks like it's approaching a limit. Running a single simulation at the center of the blob with a max period of 500,000 still fails to find a period. And what does the simulation look like?

<img src="resources/images/blog/17-4.gif" style="width: 50%;" />

It looks periodic, and yet... thinking about it more, it almost can't be. With each cycle, the right ball rebounds with a slightly different trajectory. (Did the imperfect loop of the GIF give it away?) Something about the particular angles of the platforms creates a self-correcting effect that gives the pattern (infinite?) staying power.

It makes sense that an aperiodic simulation would look like this. Patterns of bounces that change all the time will eventually miss a critical collision, collapse, then repeat. Patterns like this, that are almost the same every time, have a method to their madness that could convince you that they'll repeat, slightly differently, forever.

An interesting ramification raised by this type of pattern: you can imagine one or more configurations in this region, with precisely calculated irrational coordinates, that cause the ball to rebound in exactly the same way every time. Meaning: a 0- or 1-dimensional period-1 region may exist inside the blob. I'll let someone else find it.

#### All positive integer periods are represented

Let's zoom in on the southeast border of the same black blob and watch the "frontier" slowly advance as we increase the max period one at a time:

<img src="resources/images/blog/17-5.gif" style="width: 50%;" />
<div class="blogcaption">max detectable period 25&nbsp;&rarr; 50</div>

Weird! I expected a straightforward gradient of increasing values, as you might see with some parts of a Mandelbrot fractal. What's clear, though, is that every time we look for the next period, we see several wispy bands emerge: evidence of the representation of all periods. Once again, this is far from a proof.

## Event-driven simulation

The classic approach to simulating collisions, and the one that Box2D uses by default, is to move everything *veeery slooooowly*, in timesteps as small as you can stomach. By the time you notice that two objects have collided — uh oh! They're already intersecting each other. At least you were moving them slowly, so they're not intersecting by that much. You can then push them apart and bounce them off each other.

Which means that the collisions aren't all that accurate, kinda defeating the point of rendering a fractal that exposes hidden patterns in the natural universe which is, of course, what I'm trying to do here. If you want to make it more accurate, not only do you have to worry about [floating-point errors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating-point_error_mitigation), you also need to further shrink your timesteps, making it that much more computationally intensive to simulate. For simulations that feature continuously changing forces like the [three-body problem](https://trisolarchaos.com) or [double pendulums](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtjb2OhEQcU), there's not much you can do about that.

But there is much *we* can do about that. Our simulation features gravity, yes, but the only time the forces acting on our juggling balls *change* is when they collide with segments with and each other. That means we have a finite number of events to simulate, with relatively simple equations describing both their motion and their distance to each other. Rather than step forward in teeny-tiny timesteps, we can:

* calculate the exact future time each pair of objects will collide,
* skip immediately to the most imminent collision,
* update the position and velocity of each ball to its position at that time,
* perform the collision, and
* repeat.

<img src="resources/images/blog/17-d0.png" style="width: 50%;"/>

Calculating future collision times goes like this: say you've got the two balls above with some initial position and velocity. (Luckily, we get to ignore gravity because it accelerates both balls the same.) You can compute the distance between them at any point *t*. There are some gotchas. Calculating the distance between a falling ball and a static line segment is harder than ball-to-ball. You also have to reject negative and imaginary solutions, taking floating-point error into account. That's all worth it, though, because by finding collisions instead of waiting around for them, we avoid simulating all that time where nothing is happening.

I tried (for years, actually) to avoid floating-point numbers altogether, and instead to use these formulas and various [CASes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_algebra_system) to perform all-symbolic calculations. After just a couple collisions, the expressions describing the positions and velocities of the balls got so gigantic that no CAS could reliably tell me whether one was larger than another, if they had imaginary components, or even if they were positive. Maybe one day these CASes will get smarter and I'll build a supercomputer to perform some symbolic physics.
